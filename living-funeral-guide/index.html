Living Funeral Guide

A living funeral, sometimes called a living wake, is a planned gathering that gives someone the experience of their own memorial while they're still here to take part in it. This guide walks through how to plan one with care.

What is a living funeral?

A living funeral borrows the structure of a traditional memorial, people gather, speak, remember, and honor, but the guest of honor is present and participating. They can respond to what's shared, tell their own stories, and say their own goodbyes.

When to hold one

Timing is the most sensitive part. A living funeral is most meaningful when the honoree still has the energy to engage with it. Families navigating a terminal illness often find that scheduling it earlier rather than later is wiser, so that fatigue doesn't overshadow the day.

Planning step by step

1. Talk with the guest of honor

This event is for them, so their wishes lead. Some people want a big celebration; others want something small and intimate. Ask what would feel good, not what tradition expects.

2. Choose a setting

A home, a garden, a community room, or a favorite restaurant can all work. Comfort and accessibility matter more than formality.

3. Decide on a flow

Many living funerals include a mix of open sharing, planned speakers, music, and food. Consider whether the honoree wants to speak, and build in rest.

4. Include people who can't attend

Not everyone will be able to travel. Collecting video messages or letters ahead of time, or setting up an online tribute page, lets distant loved ones be part of the day.

Platforms like TributeWell let families create a collaborative online tribute where anyone can add photos, videos, and messages from anywhere, and revisit them for years.

5. Capture the day

Record the tributes. These recordings become a keepsake for the honoree and, later, for the family.

Making it comfortable, not somber

The word 'funeral' can feel heavy. Many families deliberately frame the day as a celebration, with laughter and storytelling encouraged. The honoree sets the tone.
